*Excerpt from my in-progress book, **Blue to Blue: Death, Consciousness, and What Humanity Invents to Live**, a work of philosophy and poetry exploring mortality, consciousness, identity, and the ways people search for meaning in finite lives. The reflection and accompanying poem appear together in the manuscript.*

Memory feels like the evidence of selfhood. We know who we are because we remember being who we were. A name means nothing without recollection. A childhood photograph seems to prove continuity only because someone can say: that was me.

Yet memory is fragile. It is incomplete, edited, biological, and often wrong. Most people do not remember infancy. Many memories blur or rearrange themselves. Trauma can fracture memory. Dementia can erase names, faces, houses, children. Brain injury can alter personality. Sleep interrupts consciousness every night. Anesthesia can extinguish awareness completely for a time.

And still, people feel continuous.

This makes identity stranger than it first appears. If the two-year-old self is inaccessible, did that self die? If a person with dementia cannot remember their past, are they no longer the person who lived it? If a future version of you forgets almost everything you now care about, is that future being still you?

A strict memory theory gives one kind of answer: sameness depends on psychological continuity. But life resists strictness. We do not stop loving someone the moment they forget. We do not treat every forgotten childhood as a dead stranger. Human identity is not only memory. It is also body, relation, care, and continuity of living process.

Still, the fear remains. If an afterlife, reincarnation, or spiritual continuation does not preserve memory, it may not preserve the self we are emotionally trying to save.

A soul without memory may satisfy metaphysics. It may not satisfy grief.

### The Two-Year-Old Is Gone

<div class="poem" style="white-space: pre-line;">
In the album, pressed against a page
gone tacky with old glue,
a handprint the size of a mouse.
Blue paint. Thumb smeared sideways.
Someone wrote a name and a year
in cursive I cannot enter.

No one remembers the afternoon,
the paint, the paper,
the small hand pressing itself into proof.
The child who made this handprint
does not exist.
Not dead. Just not here.

I am the body that hand belonged to,
but I am not the blue-fingered child
squinting at a camera
someone aimed from the hips.
That child did not know what years meant.
They thought the sun would always
come back behind the same cloud.
They were wrong about almost everything,
including themselves.

I look at the handprint and feel nothing
except the strange politeness of recognition:
that was me,
as if I am identifying a cousin
seen once at a funeral.

The gap between that blue hand
and this dry one
is not memory.
It is time.
And time is only forgetting
with better manners.
Is this a small death?
When the two-year-old vanishes
and the older self takes their place
without ceremony, without grave,
without even a ribbon of grief,
does the universe owe that child an afterlife?
Does the older self owe that child a prayer?
I hold the photograph
and try to summon them back.
The voice. The fear of the vacuum.
The way they bit their lower lip
while stacking blocks.
Nothing comes.

The paint is still blue.
The hand is no hand I remember wearing.
If the soul is the keeper of all selves,
then my soul has lost its keys.
If I am the same person without them,
then same is a word that carries too much.

And if forgetting is death,
I have died so many times already
that heaven would be a room
full of children I cannot name.
</div>
