<div class="poem" style="white-space: pre-line;">
The sky is cloudy during wars.
Not the soft, wandering white of peaceful afternoons—
but heavy, low, bruised red,
pressed down like a sleeve on an old wound.

The light bleeds through in dirty streaks,
thin and sickly,
as if the sun has forgotten
how to stand upright.

Clouds do not drift.
They gather, huddle, thicken—
a slow-motion avalanche
without sound.
They carry no rain, only weight,
and marks no weather made,
only the memory of smoke
from places that no longer have names.

Bombers slide through that ceiling,
small and hard and certain.
The clouds swallow their sound,
then give it back
as a rumble without shape,
a trembling in the teeth.

Birds do not rise.
There is no reason to climb
into that pressed, suffocating blanket.
Even the wind holds its breath
between shell blasts.

And when the fighting stops
for an hour, a day,
the clouds remain.
They have become the sky's only habit—
red upon red,
a ceiling with no door.

The blue is still there somewhere.
But during wars,
no one believes in what they cannot see.
</div>
