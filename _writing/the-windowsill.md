*Written for an English assignment in March 2026. An allegorical piece about parental expectations, the desire for safety, and the risks of choosing independence.*

My mother's hands were always precise. When she folded the paper, her fingers pressed each crease firmly, smoothing it again and again until the shape obeyed. The paper bird took form on the kitchen table beneath the window, its wings sharp and even, its body clean and symmetrical. She placed it carefully on the windowsill, where the afternoon light caught along its edges and made it glow.

"There," she said softly, more to herself than to me. "Perfect."

From the sill, the bird could see everything. Cars passed below, tires hissing against the pavement after rain. Wind moved through the trees across the street, shaking loose yellow leaves that spun before settling. Inside, the house was still. The air smelled faintly of dust and laundry detergent. Nothing moved unless it was meant to.

The bird's purpose was clear: to watch, to remain, to be kept. It was safe there. It would never be lost, never torn, never misunderstood. My mother liked to glance at it as she passed, reassured by its stillness.

But seasons have a way of shifting even the quietest rooms. As autumn deepened, the window grew cold at night. The glass hummed faintly when the wind picked up, and sometimes the bird trembled where it stood. A restlessness crept into it. It was small at first, like a draft slipping under a door, then stronger, insistent.

The windowsill began to feel less like a perch and more like a line.

The bird watched other things now. Not just the world as it passed, but the space beyond the glass, the open air, unpredictable and wide. It imagined what it would feel like to move instead of observe. The thought was thrilling and terrifying all at once.

Fear came quickly. Fear of the fall. Fear of tearing apart before ever touching the ground. Fear of my mother's face if she looked up and saw the sill empty.

What if the bird failed?

What if it crumpled, ruined, unrecognizable?

But beneath that fear lay another, heavier one: the fear of staying. The fear of remaining folded, kept, admired for a stillness that was never alive.

One afternoon, when the house was quiet and the sky stretched blue and unbroken, the decision arrived without ceremony. The window was open just enough for cold air to slip in. The bird leaned forward.

The wind was nothing like it had imagined. It was loud and sudden, grabbing instead of lifting. The bird spun, struck a rusted wire, scraped against brick. Its wings bent where they had once been exact. A corner tore. It fell hard.

When it landed, it was no longer neat. Creases ran the wrong way. The symmetry was gone.

But it was real.

Later, when I look back at that windowsill, I understand my mother's hands better. She was trying to protect what she shaped. She wanted permanence. She wanted certainty. She wanted a daughter who would stay exactly as she had made her.

But I am not a paper doll meant to stay intact on a ledge.

I am a woman who chose the air.
