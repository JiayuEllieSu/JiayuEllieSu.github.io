*Written in September 2026 for an in-class English reflection on mediating structures and how they shape our experience of the world, in response to Zadie Smith’s essay “Some Notes on Mediated Time” from Dead and Alive: Essays (2025). Lightly revised afterward.*

We as humans have always experienced time through mediating structures: religion, storytelling, politics, art, and particularly the practice of learning. While reading Smith, I was struck by the fact that although she writes primarily about social media and smartphones, AI seems like the next version of the same problem. It is not simply changing how we spend our time, but also how we perceive its value. AI promises to save us time, yet I wonder whether, in saving time now, we are stealing something from our future selves.

For roughly the first quarter of our lives, we spend much of our time receiving an education. We study ideas that humans have developed over thousands of years, whether in the sciences, the arts, or the humanities. We read texts, solve problems, and learn from those who came before us, hopefully developing the skills to eventually produce ideas of our own. Through this process, we are often evaluated on the final results of our learning: a solution, a paper, or a presentation.

But now, when an LLM can write the paper, provide the solution, or generate the presentation, what are we really being evaluated on?

The value of education is not solely in the final product. When we struggle to solve a problem, we are developing the ability to approach problems we have never encountered before. When we write a paper, we are not merely arranging sentences into paragraphs, but learning to organize our thoughts, question our assumptions, and construct our own arguments. An LLM might produce a polished result in seconds, but the time we would have spent struggling toward that result is not necessarily wasted. In many ways, that time *is* the education.

Smith discusses the idea of time being money in today's world. The time we invest in our education is often understood in terms of its eventual return, whether through grades, college admissions, or future salaries. If AI allows us to produce better results in less time, it seems, at first, like an obvious advantage. But what if the time we save comes at the expense of the very skills our education is supposed to develop? We might gain a better grade or a better opportunity now, only to discover years later that we never learned how to solve an unfamiliar problem without assistance. In that sense, we are not saving time at all. We are borrowing it from our future selves.

Of course, not every struggle is equally valuable. I personally use LLMs as a student, but generally not to produce my final work. I use them to help with tasks I already know how to do, such as fixing LaTeX formatting in a research paper, organizing files on my computer, or correcting syntax errors in my code. These tasks can consume time without necessarily contributing much to my understanding. Yet the distinction is not always obvious. Debugging code, for instance, can be a mechanical inconvenience, but it can also reveal something important about the logic of a program. Perhaps the question is not whether AI eliminates struggle, but *which struggles we are choosing to eliminate*. AI should give us more time to engage with the ideas that matter, not spare us from having to engage with them at all.

For the remaining three quarters of our lives, we continue learning, but we also spend much of our time applying the skills we have developed. This is where I see a second problem emerging. AI has changed not only how quickly we can produce work, but also what we consider a reasonable amount of work to produce.

If an AI can read through papers, draft texts, organize data, write code, and generate ideas faster than a human can alone, what happens to all the time we supposedly save? Do we actually get that time back, or are we simply expected to produce more?

I have personally worked on two substantial scientific research projects, and I have begun to notice this tension in my own expectations. Scientific research has always been a slow process of reading, experimenting, failing, revising, and occasionally discovering something new. But with AI tools becoming increasingly capable, the pace of what seems possible is changing. Tasks that might once have required hours of routine work can now be completed much faster. At the same time, I find myself becoming more conscious of how quickly I am progressing, especially when AI systems are advancing in the very fields I am trying to understand.

This has changed my perception of productivity and, in turn, my perception of time. Something that once seemed like a reasonable amount of progress can suddenly feel insufficient. If a tool allows me to accomplish more in a day, I begin to expect more of myself the next day. The time saved does not necessarily become free time. Instead, it becomes another opportunity to accomplish something.

And this makes me wonder what we are actually gaining. Scientific research is not valuable only because it produces papers or solutions. The process of encountering something unfamiliar and gradually learning to understand it is itself an important part of becoming a researcher. My current projects are not merely contributions I hope to make to their respective fields. They are also exercises in developing the skills and intuition I will need to approach more difficult problems in the future. If I become so focused on producing results that I rush through that process, I risk losing something that cannot be measured by the number of papers I publish or problems I solve.

There is a strange parallel between these two stages of life. During our education, AI can tempt us to skip the process of developing our abilities in order to reach a result more quickly. Later, in our work, it can raise expectations for how quickly those abilities should produce results. In both cases, we begin treating time spent thinking, struggling, and learning as an inefficiency to be eliminated.

Perhaps this is what concerns me most about AI as a mediating structure. It does not merely allow us to do things faster. It can change our expectations of what time should be used for in the first place. When every task can be accelerated, the moments that resist acceleration begin to feel unproductive, even when those moments are the ones in which we learn the most.

I do believe AI can help us save time if we use it thoughtfully. But we need to be careful about the boundary between a tool that supports our thinking and a system that begins to determine what we expect from ourselves. The ability to struggle through uncertainty, to spend time on a question without knowing when we will find an answer, is part of what allows us to develop ideas that are genuinely our own.

AI can give us back hours of our lives. But if those hours are immediately filled with greater expectations, or if we save them by surrendering the experiences through which we grow, have we really gained any time at all?
