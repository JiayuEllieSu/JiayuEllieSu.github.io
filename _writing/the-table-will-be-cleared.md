*I wrote this during a summer math program after attending a student lecture on the history of mathematics. The lecture inspired me to reflect on the Scottish Café, the fragility of mathematical knowledge, and why mathematics can matter even when nothing lasts forever.*

Every night, the equations disappeared.

At the Scottish Café in Lwów, Poland, mathematicians wrote directly on the marble tabletops with soft pencils. Stefan Banach, Stanisław Mazur, Stanisław Ulam, Herman Auerbach, Hugo Steinhaus, and others would sit for hours over coffee and cognac, arguing about functional analysis, topology, probability, and set theory. Sometimes a session lasted through most of the night. Then the café closed. The chairs were turned upside down, the floor was swept, and a cleaner wiped the tables. By morning, an idea that had taken seventeen hours to develop might have vanished.

At first, the mathematicians tried to preserve their work by asking the café staff not to wash certain tables. Eventually, Banach’s wife, Lucja, bought a thick notebook with a marbled cover. Problems were written on one page and their solutions, when anyone found them, on the facing page. The notebook became the Scottish Book. It contained 193 numbered problems and several additional ones, with prizes that included wine, beer, and, for one problem, a live goose.

I keep returning to the image of those tables being cleared.

The usual answer to the question “Why do mathematics?” is that mathematics advances human knowledge. It allows us to describe nature, build technologies, and identify structures that would otherwise remain invisible. A theorem can become the foundation of another theorem, then part of a theory, then the basis of an application its creator never anticipated. The work of the mathematicians in Lwów eventually reached probability, computing, game theory, physics, cryptography, and medical imaging. Their conversations did matter far beyond the café.

But that answer has never felt complete to me.

To say that we do mathematics in order to advance knowledge simply moves the question back one step. Why must knowledge be advanced? What is the final purpose of building an increasingly elaborate structure of understanding if the people capable of understanding it will not exist forever?

Humanity may last for thousands or millions of years, but not indefinitely. Given enough time, every notebook will decay, every theorem will be forgotten, and every mind capable of recognizing a proof will disappear. The distinction between an idea remembered for ten years and one remembered for ten thousand years becomes small when measured against the lifetime of the universe. Eventually, there may be no one left to know that mathematics ever existed.

This thought does not make me want to stop doing mathematics. It makes me suspicious of any explanation that depends on permanence.

During the program, my lecturer, Timothy All, described mathematics as a way of honoring the human spirit. We do it, in some sense, because we can.

I initially thought “because we can” sounded almost dismissive. It seemed weaker than saying that mathematics improves civilization or reveals universal truth. But I have come to think that it may be the more fundamental answer. Human beings possess the ability to ask questions that are not forced upon us by immediate survival. We can imagine objects that have no physical form, reason about dimensions we cannot see, and establish conclusions about infinite structures using finite arguments. We can begin with a definition and discover that it contains consequences no one noticed before.

To do mathematics is to use that capacity fully.

Some people may not need a philosophy for this. A problem is placed in front of them, they want to solve it, and that desire is sufficient. There is no distance between the activity and its justification. I understand that position. Perhaps it is even the most honest one.

For me, however, the question remains. I want to know why the experience of understanding something can feel so significant when I also know that neither I nor anything I prove will last forever.

The phrase “honoring the human spirit” gives me an answer, but not because it assigns mathematics some grand external mission. It does almost the opposite. It suggests that mathematics does not have to justify itself by becoming permanent, profitable, or historically important. The act of understanding can have value while it is happening. A finite mind can still be used well.

The Scottish Café made this idea concrete for me because mathematics there was inseparable from ordinary human life. The people around its tables were not the clean, detached names that appear above theorems in textbooks. They arrived late, argued, played chess, smoked, drank too much coffee, and sometimes stayed for hours. Banach disliked formality and could work almost anywhere. Noise did not disturb him. Mazur and Ulam brought problems to the table. Auerbach believed that chaos could be mathematically productive. They worked in a café that was also occupied by philosophers, artists, journalists, musicians, and people simply passing through the city.

The mathematics was serious, but it was not solemn.

A problem could be written into the Scottish Book beside the promise of a bottle of wine. Mazur offered a live goose for the solution of Problem 153, and decades later he actually presented it to Per Enflo after Enflo solved the problem. The humor did not diminish the mathematics. It made clear that mathematics was not an abstract monument standing above life. It was something people did together.

That is what most textbooks conceal.

A theorem on a page appears inevitable. Its assumptions are listed cleanly. Its proof proceeds in the correct order. No trace remains of the hours when the right definition was unclear, the first approach failed, or the central idea had not yet appeared. The final proof creates the illusion that mathematics has always existed in finished form.

Learning about mathematicians breaks that illusion. Someone had to decide which question was worth asking. Someone had to invent the language in which the question could be expressed. Someone had to follow a false route far enough to understand why it failed. Before a theorem became a theorem, it was uncertainty in another person’s mind.

This is one reason the history of mathematics makes me want to become a mathematician. It turns a body of results into a human conversation.

A Banach space is not only a definition. Behind the name is Banach sitting in a noisy café, working with almost total concentration. The Banach-Steinhaus theorem is not only a result in functional analysis. Behind it are collaborators who met because Steinhaus overheard Banach discussing the Lebesgue integral in a park and recognized that he was extraordinary. Schauder is not only the name of a fixed-point theorem. He was a person with a wife and daughter, limited time, financial pressures, and mathematical ideas he was still trying to develop.

Susana H. Case’s poems about the Scottish Café place these mathematical lives beside beer glasses, raisins, railway stations, lice cages, deportations, and fear. Her poems refuse to allow the mathematicians to become symbols of pure intellect. Their ideas existed inside vulnerable bodies and unstable historical circumstances.

That matters because the story of the Scottish Café is not ultimately a comforting story about genius.

The last numbered entry in the Scottish Book was written by Steinhaus on May 31, 1941. It asks about a mathematician who carries two matchboxes, chooses one at random whenever he needs a match, and eventually discovers one box empty. The problem asks for the distribution of the number of matches remaining in the other box.

It is elegant and almost playful. A mathematician reaches into a pocket. One box is empty. How many matches remain in the other?

A few weeks later, Germany invaded Soviet-controlled Lwów.

The mathematical community was broken apart. Some members emigrated. Some went into hiding. Some were murdered in ghettos, camps, or mass executions. Forty-five professors from Lwów’s university and polytechnic were killed. Schauder was murdered. Auerbach died during the Holocaust. Banach survived by working as a lice feeder at the Weigl Institute, allowing lice used in typhus-vaccine research to feed on his blood. Ulam had left for the United States shortly before the war and later worked on the Monte Carlo method, early computers, and the Manhattan Project.

The Scottish Book survived.

It would be easy to turn this into a simple claim that mathematics defeated history. The people died, but their ideas lived on. The notebook endured, and therefore the work achieved immortality.

I do not think that is the right conclusion.

A preserved theorem cannot restore a murdered person. A mathematical legacy does not compensate for a life that was taken. The survival of the book did not undo the destruction of the community that created it. Many of the people whose handwriting appeared on its pages never returned to the café.

What matters is not that mathematics escaped loss. It did not. What matters is that the work remained possible after loss.

Lucja Banach carried the Scottish Book to Wrocław after the war. There, surviving mathematicians began a New Scottish Book. Steinhaus, who had entered the final problem in the old volume, contributed at the beginning of the new one. The new book did not reconstruct the vanished world of Lwów. It did not close the rupture. It simply continued.

That continuation is closer to what I understand as the human spirit.

It is not the belief that human beings can defeat time. It is the decision to think without requiring a guarantee that the thought will last. It is the willingness to care about a problem even when its eventual importance is unknown. It is the act of handing a question to another person and leaving room for an answer.

Perhaps purpose is not something the universe provides. Perhaps it is something people create by deciding what deserves their attention. “Advancing knowledge” is one such purpose. It is real because generations of people have chosen to make it real. The fact that we created it does not make it false. It means that meaning belongs to human activity rather than existing as an external reward for it.

When I work on mathematics, the part I value most often occurs before the proof is finished. I know that a structure is present, but I cannot yet see it. I try one approach and discover that it cannot work. I return to a definition I thought I understood. A condition that seemed technical becomes essential. Two facts that appeared unrelated begin to constrain each other.

Then, occasionally, the problem reorganizes itself.

Nothing physical changes. The room is the same. The statement is the same. The theorem was true before I understood it. Yet something that had been inaccessible becomes necessary. I can see not only that the conclusion is true, but why it could not have been otherwise.

That transition is one of the clearest experiences of meaning I know.

It does not depend on whether the problem is famous. It does not require the result to be useful. It may not advance mathematics in any objective sense. Often, I am rediscovering something that thousands of people already know. But the understanding is new within one mind, and that is not nothing.

Mathematics trains a particular relationship with uncertainty. Confusion is not always evidence that a problem is beyond reach. Sometimes it means that the right distinction has not yet been made. A failed approach is not empty if it reveals what the structure will not allow. A proof is not merely an answer. It is an account of why other possibilities have been eliminated.

This experience can also pass between people. A proof is not private certainty. It is something another person can inspect, challenge, and eventually understand from within. When I read an old argument carefully enough to see why each step was chosen, I am not simply collecting its conclusion. I am temporarily following another person’s way of seeing.

That is how mathematics survives, not as an immortal object, but as an activity repeatedly reconstructed in individual minds.

I want to become a mathematician because I want to participate in that reconstruction. I want to ask questions whose answers are not yet organized, learn how to separate a promising idea from a seductive mistake, and write arguments exact enough that someone else can depend on them. I want to understand the work that came before me well enough to continue it, even if only slightly.

I do not expect anything I prove to survive for a billion years. I do not know whether my work will be useful, remembered, or important. Those cannot be the conditions under which I begin.

The mathematicians of the Scottish Café did not know which of their conversations would influence computing, probability, or physics. They did not know which problems would remain open or which of their names would still be spoken. They arrived, argued, wrote, erased, and returned the next day because the questions held their attention.

The universe offers no guarantee that their work, or ours, will be preserved.

But permanence is not the only form of value.

For now, there are minds capable of asking for reasons and recognizing when those reasons are sufficient. There are problems on the left-hand page and blank space on the right. There are questions another person began and did not finish.

The table will be cleared.

I want to write on it anyway.
