*Excerpt from my in-progress book, **Blue to Blue: Death, Consciousness, and What Humanity Invents to Live**, a work of philosophy and poetry exploring mortality, consciousness, identity, and the ways people search for meaning in finite lives.*

Legacy is often offered as a secular afterlife. Write a book. Raise children. Change a life. Build something useful. Leave the world different than it was.

This is noble, but not absolute.

The problem is scale. On human time, legacy matters deeply. A teacher may alter a student's life. A parent may shape generations. A poem may enter a stranger's mind years after its writer dies. A scientific discovery may change medicine, technology, or the story humanity tells about reality.

But cosmic time is merciless. Languages vanish. Libraries burn. Civilizations become ruins. Stars exhaust their fuel. Planets become uninhabitable. Even if humanity survives for a long time, it remains finite inside larger physical processes.

So legacy cannot be the whole answer if the demand is eternity.

But perhaps eternity was the wrong demand. A thing does not need to last forever in order to matter within the scale where it occurs. A song ending does not make the song fake. A conversation forgotten by history can still rescue the person who heard it. A child comforted at night is not meaningless because the Sun will one day die.

The failure of forever forces a different kind of meaning: not permanence, but participation.
